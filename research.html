Job Market Paper

Mobile Internet and Parental Time Investment in Children

Read abstract

Parental time is a critical input in child human capital production, yet little is known about how the rapid diffusion of mobile internet — which provides constant, zero-marginal-cost access to information and entertainment — has affected it. The effect is theoretically ambiguous: mobile internet may distract parents from active engagement with their children, or it may free up time through efficiency gains in shopping, scheduling, and remote work, allowing more focused time with children. I provide the first causal evidence on this question by exploiting the staggered geographic rollout of 3G/4G networks across U.S. metropolitan areas, combining time-diary data from the American Time Use Survey with the Collins Bartholomew GSMA Mobile Coverage Database. Because network rollout timing was driven by infrastructure and spectrum constraints rather than local demand for parenting outcomes, this provides plausibly exogenous variation. Using the Callaway and Sant’Anna (2021) difference-in-differences estimator, I find that 3G/4G coverage increases total daily childcare time by 15%, driven entirely by an increase in secondary (passive) childcare, while active childcare is unaffected. The paper further documents heterogeneity by gender and education and decomposes secondary childcare by concurrent activity to identify which specific behaviors mobile internet displaces.

Low Birthweight and Child's Achievement: Does Pre-K Compensate?

Read abstract

Children born with low birthweight (LBW) often face greater difficulty developing cognitive skills, and early interventions such as pre-kindergarten (pre-K) are often proposed as remediation. Using an instrumental-variable strategy based on heteroskedasticity and data from the Early Childhood Longitudinal Study, Kindergarten Class of 2010–11 (ECLS-K:2011), I test whether pre-K attendance compensates for the academic disadvantages associated with LBW. Low birthweight exerts a persistent, negative direct effect on standardized reading and math test scores throughout elementary school, with larger penalties for mathematics. Pre-K does not mediate the relationship between birthweight and achievement — suggesting other channels are needed to close this gap. Heterogeneity analyses reveal that the direct effect is substantially larger among low-income families, and there is a positive indirect effect through pre-K, though imprecisely estimated.

Changing Course: Robots and Adolescent Human Capital Investment

with Wookun Kim

Read abstract

We study how automation in local labor markets shapes adolescents' human capital investment. Drawing on administrative records covering the universe of Texas public high school students, we construct a county-level panel of schooling outcomes spanning 1993 to 2018 and estimate the causal effects of local exposure to industrial robot adoption, exploiting quasi-experimental variation in the timing and intensity of robotization across counties. Robot exposure reduces absenteeism in every grade, raises graduation among seniors, slows the statewide increase in grade-12 CTE participation, and increases advanced-course enrollment and math and science coursework in the upper grades. Matched payroll records show these responses are not driven by declines in student employment. Both boys and girls are drawn into school---girls account for the gains in retention, boys for the larger shift into math and science---and the curricular responses systematically narrow pre-existing gender gaps in what students study. The human capital adjustment to automation begins before labor market entry.

The Impact of Pre-K on Socioemotional and Cognitive Skills

Draft coming soon

Read abstract

Pre-kindergarten is often a child's first shared school environment, and a large literature documents its benefits for later cognitive performance. Evidence on its relationship with socioemotional ("soft") skill development is far more limited. This paper measures the average impact of pre-K attendance on both soft and cognitive skills for children in seventh grade, using primary data collected by the Joaquim Nabuco Foundation in 2018 in randomized public schools in Recife, Brazil. Soft skills are measured with the SENNA instrument; cognitive skills are measured with mathematics and literacy test scores. I estimate effects using a two-stage least squares approach within an IV-SLX model, instrumenting for pre-K attendance using indirect friends' pre-K participation. Beyond a positive effect on test scores, I find pre-K attendance positively affects conscientiousness and assertiveness.
